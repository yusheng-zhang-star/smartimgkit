Phone product photos are typically 5-12MB JPGs at 4000x3000 resolution — far larger than any store needs, and heavy enough to push mobile page loads past 10 seconds. Resizing to the actual display dimensions and compressing thoughtfully removes most of that weight with no visible difference. Most product photos straight from your phone are 5-12MB JPG files, taken at 4000x3000 resolution from a 12MP+ camera. The fix: compress them — but without making them look blurry.

The sweet spot for image compression

Here's what most people get wrong: they think "compression = blurry images." But modern compression algorithms are smarter than that. The human eye literally cannot tell the difference between a JPG saved at 100% quality and one saved at 75% quality — but the file size difference is massive.

  • JPG at 100% quality: 10MB (there is never a reason to use this for web)
  • JPG at 80% quality: 2.5MB (great, no visible loss at all)
  • JPG at 70% quality: 1.2MB (perfect for web, still looks crisp on retina screens)
  • JPG at 50% quality: 400KB (starts showing compression artifacts when you zoom in)
Our free image compressor defaults to exactly this 75% quality setting, so you get maximum file size reduction without any visible quality loss. It runs locally in your browser, so your photos never get uploaded to any remote server.

Why WebP is even better

If you're willing to go one step further, convert your product photos to WebP format. At the exact same visual quality:

  • WebP files are 25-35% smaller than equivalent JPGs
  • WebP files are 25% smaller than equivalent PNGs
  • Every browser made after 2020 supports WebP — which covers 97% of global internet users
For an ecommerce store with 20 product images per page, switching to WebP cuts total page image weight from 15MB to 6MB — a massive difference for mobile load times, especially on slower 4G connections. Our JPG to WebP converter does this conversion locally, no upload required.

The correct compression order most people get wrong

Compressing your images isn't just sliding a quality slider — the order you do things matters a lot. The correct workflow is:

  1. Resize first: Shrink your 4000x3000 photo to 2000px wide — this alone cuts file size by 75%.
  2. Convert format: Switch from PNG/HEIC to WebP/JPG for another 30% size reduction.
  3. Adjust quality last: Set to 75% quality for the final 30% cut.
If you compress the original 4000px photo at 75% quality first, you end up with a 2MB file — but if you resize it to 2000px first, then compress, you end up with a 200KB file at identical visible quality. That's a 10x difference.

Common compression mistakes to avoid

  • Don't over-compress detailed products: If your product photos have tiny text, labels, or fine details (like jewelry, electronics, fabric texture), bump quality up to 80% to keep edges sharp and avoid blurry text.
  • Don't upload PNGs for photos: PNG is for screenshots, logos, and graphics with transparent backgrounds. For regular product photos, JPG or WebP is 5-10x smaller at the same quality — using PNG for photos just bloats your page for no reason.
  • Don't skip resizing: As above, a 4000×3000 photo resized to 2000×2000px and compressed is 90% smaller than just compressing the original. Always resize before compressing.

How to tell if your compression is too aggressive

It can be hard to tell at a glance if you've compressed an image too much, because compression artifacts are subtle when you're looking at the image at normal screen size. The easiest way to check is to zoom in to 100% on the most detailed part of the photo: look at edges around text, product logos, fine fabric texture, or sharp lines. If you see fuzzy halos around edges, blocky color patches in smooth gradients (like sky or wall backgrounds), or text that looks soft and out of focus, you've compressed too hard, and you need to bump the quality slider up 5-10%. If everything looks sharp and clean at 100% zoom, your compression is fine, and the file size is as small as it can be without sacrificing visible quality. You don't need to be a professional photographer to tell the difference — once you see what over-compression looks like, it's obvious, and you'll know exactly what to avoid.

For people who work with screenshots, the compression rules are a little different. Screenshots with sharp text, UI elements, and flat colors need higher quality settings than regular photos, because JPEG compression is especially bad at rendering sharp text and hard edges — it creates ugly halos around letters that make screenshots look unprofessional. For screenshots, use 85% quality or export as PNG, which is lossless for flat graphics. If you're converting screenshots to WebP, you can go even lower (around 80%) because WebP handles sharp edges much better than JPEG, and you'll still get crisp text at a smaller file size. A lot of bloggers and developers ruin their tutorial screenshots by compressing them too hard, which makes their whole post look low-effort and unpolished — taking 2 extra seconds to check that text is sharp after compression makes a huge difference in how professional your content looks.

Another thing people don't realize: every time you re-save a JPG, you lose a little bit of quality, even if you set the quality slider to 100%. That's because JPEG is a lossy format — every save throws away some visual data to make the file smaller, and those small losses add up over multiple edits. That means you should always keep your original unedited master files, and only make compressed copies for web use, never edit and re-save the same JPG over and over. If you need to edit an image, start from the original master, make your edits, then export a new compressed web version, instead of opening the already-compressed web JPG, editing it, and saving it again. That habit alone will keep your images looking sharp for years, and you won't end up with a folder full of degraded, blurry JPGs that you can't trace back to the original.

FAQ

Is WebP really better than JPG for file size? Yes. WebP at equivalent visual quality produces files 25-35% smaller than JPG, and 25% smaller than PNG. All modern browsers support WebP, so there's no downside to switching.

Does compressing images hurt my Google rankings? Actually, the opposite. Large, uncompressed images slow down your site, which hurts Core Web Vitals. Properly compressed images improve your LCP score and boost rankings.

Should I resize before compressing? Yes. A 4000x3000 photo resized to 2000x2000px then compressed is 90% smaller than just compressing the original at full resolution. Always resize first.

Why are my phone photos so big? Modern phones shoot 12MP+ photos at 4000x3000px resolution, which creates 5-12MB files. Web pages only need images 2000px wide, so the extra resolution is wasted weight.
