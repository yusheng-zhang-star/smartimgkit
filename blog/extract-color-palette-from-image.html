Tips for Better Color Extraction

  • Use a clean product photo: If your photo has a busy background, the extractor will pick up background colors instead of product colors. For best results, use a cutout product photo on white background — use our background remover to cut out the product first.
  • Pick the right reference image: If your product has multiple colors (e.g. a colorful mug), upload the image with the lighting you actually sell under. Colors look different under warm vs cool light, so pick the photo that matches your real product.
  • Use the dominant colors, not every color: The 6 extracted swatches are the most common colors in the image. Don't try to use every tiny accent color in your branding — stick to 2-3 main colors for a clean look.
  • Test contrast: If you're using a color for text, check that it has enough contrast against your background. A dark orange on a cream background looks great, but light orange text on white is unreadable.

Common Mistakes

  • Extracting colors from filtered photos: If your product photo has an Instagram filter applied, the colors will be off. Use the original raw photo for color extraction.
  • Ignoring lighting: A photo taken in warm golden hour light will have a yellow tint. If you want true product colors, take photos in neutral daylight, then extract the palette.
  • Using too many colors: A 6-color palette is for reference, not for all 6 to be used on one page. Pick 1 main brand color, 1 secondary color, and 1 accent color.

Why local color extraction matters for brand assets

Most online color picker tools work by uploading your image to their server, running the color analysis there, and showing you the palette. That means the image you uploaded — maybe a product photo of your unreleased new line, or a client's confidential branding mockup — is sitting on their server. For design agencies and brand owners, that's a real leak risk. Local processing means the color analysis runs entirely in your browser: the image is read from your computer, the pixels are analyzed on your own CPU, and nothing gets sent anywhere. You can disconnect from the internet after the page loads and the tool still works, because there's no server involved at all. That's the only way to be sure your unreleased product photos or client work doesn't get leaked while you pull colors.

It's also faster. Cloud tools have to wait for the upload, then wait for the server to process, then send the results back — that's 5-10 seconds of waiting per image. Local analysis takes milliseconds, because it's just reading pixels on your own screen. If you're working on a design project and need to pull 10 different product palettes in a row, that difference adds up fast. No upload progress bar, no waiting, just instant results.

How this is different from a generic color wheel tool

Generic color palette generators ask you to pick a starting color and then spit out complementary shades based on mathematical color theory. Extracting colors directly from a photo captures all of that. That's the difference between a brand that looks "off" and one that feels cohesive — you're using the real colors customers see when they hold your product, not a theoretical set of complementary shades.
