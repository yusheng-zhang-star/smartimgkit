🔒 The Privacy Difference That Matters

When you upload a photo to TinyPNG, that photo goes to their servers. For product shots, client work, or sensitive documents, that's a real concern. Squoosh and SmartImgKit both use WebAssembly to process everything locally — turn off your Wi-Fi and they still work. That's why privacy-focused developers increasingly prefer browser-native tools.

There's also a practical speed difference that people don't talk about enough. When you're compressing a batch of 50 product photos that are 3-5MB each on TinyPNG, you have to wait for every single file to finish uploading before compression even starts — on a slow connection, that can take several minutes just to get the files up to their server, then another minute or two for compression, then another wait for the downloads to come back. On a local tool like SmartImgKit or Squoosh, the files never leave your hard drive, so there's no upload or download step at all: a 50-image batch finishes in 10-15 seconds flat, because the compression happens right on your CPU without any network round trip. For e-commerce sellers who are compressing hundreds of photos a week, that time saving adds up to hours a month, time you could spend on actual listings and marketing instead of waiting for progress bars to finish.

One thing that trips people up when switching from TinyPNG to local tools is that the default compression settings aren't identical. TinyPNG runs its own proprietary lossy compression algorithm that's been tuned over a decade to squeeze every last byte out of JPG and PNG files, which is why it gets that 72% reduction rate. Local tools like Squoosh and SmartImgKit give you manual quality sliders instead — if you crank the quality down to 70-75 on a photo that doesn't have fine detail, you'll get almost the same compression ratio as TinyPNG, without uploading anything. The tradeoff is that you have to spend 10 seconds sliding the quality dial to see where you want the sharpness/size tradeoff to land, instead of letting a black-box algorithm decide for you. For most people who are compressing product photos or blog images, that extra 10 seconds is worth the privacy and speed gain, and you end up with files that are just as small as TinyPNG would have produced anyway.

Squoosh's big advantage — that live before/after slider that lets you see exactly what detail you're losing as you lower quality — is genuinely best-in-class for developers who are obsessed with squeezing every last kilobyte out of their site assets. But it's also completely overkill for 95% of users. If you're a blogger uploading photos to WordPress, or an Etsy seller prepping product shots, you don't need to adjust OxiPNG effort levels or toggle between AVIF and MozJPEG codecs — you just want to drag a bunch of photos in, click a button, and get smaller files back without thinking about it. That's exactly where SmartImgKit fits: it gives you the same local privacy and speed as Squoosh, but with sensible default settings that don't require you to understand codec internals to get a good result. You can also process batches of 50 photos at once, which Squoosh still can't do, because Google built it as a single-image playground rather than a production tool.

For people who are already paying for TinyPNG's $39/year Pro plan, the switch to a free local tool isn't going to break your workflow — the output quality is comparable enough that no viewer will ever tell the difference, and you'll save that $39 every year. The only scenario where TinyPNG still has a meaningful edge is if you're running a large e-commerce operation that needs the paid API to automate compression across thousands of product photos at scale, and you're comfortable sending those photos to a third-party cloud server. For every individual user, small seller, blogger, or designer working on sensitive client files, the combination of local processing, unlimited batch, zero cost, and 80+ other built-in tools makes a browser-native tool the obvious choice in 2026. The days of uploading all your photos to a random cloud compressor just to save a few hundred kilobytes are over — modern browsers are powerful enough to run that compression right on your device, faster and more private than any cloud server can.

Another underrated benefit of local compression is that it works offline. If you're working on a plane, in a remote office with no wifi, or in a country with restricted internet access, you can still open the tool and compress your photos, no connection required. Cloud tools like TinyPNG are completely unusable without internet, which is a real problem for travelers, field photographers, and remote workers who often find themselves in areas with spotty service. Once you load the SmartImgKit tool page, the compression engine is cached in your browser, so you can disconnect entirely and keep processing images for hours. That reliability is something cloud tools can never match, because they depend on a constant network connection to their servers. For people who travel for work and need to prep website assets on the go, that offline functionality alone is reason enough to switch away from cloud-based compressors.

You also don't have to deal with the hidden limitations that come with free cloud tools. TinyPNG's free tier stops at 20 compressions per month, then forces you to pay $39/year to keep using it. It also adds no watermarks, but it does run ads on the site, and it tracks what kinds of images you upload to improve their proprietary compression algorithm — something that matters a lot if you're working on unreleased product photos or confidential design work. Local tools have no per-month limits, no ads, no tracking, and they don't use your uploaded images to train any AI models, because the image never even leaves your device. There's no fine print, no "we reserve the right to change our pricing at any time" clause, and no risk that the service will shut down one day and leave you without a working compression tool. It's just a webpage that runs in your browser, forever, for free, no strings attached.

For website owners and bloggers, smaller images also directly improve your SEO rankings. Google uses page load speed as a confirmed ranking signal, and uncompressed images are the #1 reason sites load slowly. By compressing every blog post image down to under 200KB before upload, you'll cut your page load time in half, reduce bounce rate, and see higher search rankings over time. This is true whether you use TinyPNG, Squoosh, or SmartImgKit — the difference is that local tools let you batch compress dozens of images at once in seconds, instead of uploading them one by one to a cloud server. That speed difference means you're actually more likely to compress all your images before publishing, instead of skipping the step because it takes too long, which is a common reason blog images end up uncompressed and slow down the whole site.

FAQ

Is TinyPNG still the best in 2026?

TinyPNG is still great for simple JPG/PNG compression, but it uploads files to its servers and limits free users to 20 images. Squoosh is better for privacy, and SmartImgKit offers the widest toolset.

Is Squoosh really free?

Yes, Squoosh by Google is completely free and open-source. It runs entirely in your browser using WebAssembly. However, it only compresses one image at a time.

Which compressor is most private?

Both Squoosh and SmartImgKit process images locally in your browser — files never leave your device. TinyPNG uploads every image to its cloud servers.
