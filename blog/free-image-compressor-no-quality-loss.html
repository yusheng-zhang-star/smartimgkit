Common compression mistakes that ruin quality

  • Compressing too aggressively. Sliding quality down to 30% gets you tiny files, but product photos look fuzzy and blocky on high-res phone screens — that directly hurts conversion. 75-80% is the target.
  • Compressing PNGs that already have transparency. Transparent PNGs don't compress well with JPG settings — switch to WebP instead, which cuts file size by 25-35% while keeping transparency intact.
  • Compressing photos you've already saved for web. If you've run an image through compression once, running it again adds quality loss with minimal file size gain. Start from the original, uncompressed master file every time.

How JPEG compression actually works (the short version)

If you've ever wondered why compression can shrink files so much without visible quality loss, it comes down to how the JPEG format stores color information. Human eyes are much more sensitive to brightness changes than to small color shifts — JPEG compression exploits this by throwing away tiny color variations that our brains can't pick up anyway. It also groups small blocks of pixels together and stores them as a single value, which cuts down redundant data in smooth backgrounds (like a white product backdrop) dramatically. That's why product photos on plain backgrounds compress so well — most of the file is just smooth white, which JPEG encodes extremely efficiently. Photos with busy, detailed backgrounds (like a crowded street scene) don't compress as much, because there's less redundant data to remove. That's a natural limit, not a flaw in the tool. Even on those detailed photos, you'll still see a 50%+ size reduction with no visible loss at 75% quality. You don't have to understand the codec to use it, but knowing that compression isn't "making your photo blurry" — it's just removing data your eyes would never see — takes the anxiety out of shrinking your images for the web. It also works great for shrinking images before you attach them to emails, because most email providers have strict attachment size limits, and a 10MB photo shrunk to 200KB at 75% quality looks perfectly fine in an email, while staying well under the attachment limit.
