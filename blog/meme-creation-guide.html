How to Make a Meme: Complete Beginner's Guide (No Photoshop Needed)

I spent 45 minutes trying to make one stupid meme for my team's Slack channel. The first three attempts were so bad I deleted them immediately. Here's everything I learned since then about making memes that people actually share.

Quick answer:

A meme is just an image with high-contrast text in Impact font. The magic is in the caption timing, not the tool. Make the text readable at thumbnail size and you're 80% there.

TL;DR

Good memes come from templates you can riff on, text that's short and punchy, and timing (post when your audience is awake). Bad memes try too hard to be original. The fastest workflow: find a template, type 1-2 lines of text, save, post. This guide covers the rest: which templates actually work, the typography trick that makes text readable, and the line between "funny" and "trying too hard."

1. The 45 Minutes I'll Never Get Back

Last year, my coworker posted a meme in our team Slack that referenced an inside joke from a particularly chaotic sprint planning meeting. Simple image, two lines of text. Everyone cracked up. Like, actual laughing-out-loud in the office. I thought: I want to do that.

So I opened Photoshop. Stared at a blank canvas. Realized I had no idea what dimensions memes are supposed to be. Googled "meme dimensions." Got twelve different answers. Found a meme template on some sketchy website covered in ads. Downloaded it. Opened it in Photoshop. Tried to add text. The font looked wrong. The text was too big. Then too small. Then somehow bold and italic at the same time. After 45 minutes, I had produced something that looked like a ransom note made by someone who'd never seen a computer before.

I closed Photoshop and didn't try making memes again for months.

Here's what nobody told me: you don't need Photoshop. Or any design skills. Or even a downloaded template. The tools have gotten so good that anyone can make a decent meme in under five minutes. You just need to know which tools actually work and avoid the mistakes that make memes look amateurish.

I've made probably a hundred memes since that disaster. Some went viral in my friend groups. Some flopped. I'll tell you why. This guide is what I wish I'd found back when I was googling "meme dimensions" and getting nowhere.

2. What Makes a Meme Actually Funny

Before you touch a single tool, you need to understand why some memes work and others don't. I've made enough bad ones to spot the patterns.

A good meme does one of a few things. Makes a specific observation that people recognize instantly. Subverts an expectation in a funny way. Captures a shared emotional experience. The memes that go viral in Slack channels and group chats? They almost always nail one of these.

The ones that flop? They're too vague. "Work is hard" isn't a meme — it's a statement. "Me reading the 47th email about a meeting that could've been a Slack message" is a meme. Specificity is the difference between someone scrolling past and someone sending your meme to three friends.

There's also the format factor. The template you pick shapes the joke. You wouldn't use the Distracted Boyfriend format for a sincere message, just like you wouldn't use the "This Is Fine" dog for something that isn't about barely-contained panic. The format is part of the joke.

3. Make Your First Meme in 5 Minutes

Alright, let's stop talking and actually make one. I'm going to walk you through creating a classic image macro — the top-text-bottom-text format that you've probably seen a thousand times. No downloads, no accounts, no Photoshop.

Step 1: Pick your template (or use your own image)

You can either grab a popular meme template from somewhere like Know Your Meme or Imgflip, or — and this is what I do most of the time — use your own photo. Inside jokes with your own photos hit way harder than recycled templates.

If you're using a template, just save the image to your computer. If you're taking a screenshot, the SmartImgKit Cropper is handy for trimming out browser chrome and getting just the image you want. I've accidentally left the Windows taskbar in a meme before and someone in the comments roasted me for it. Crop first. Trust me.

Step 2: Open the meme generator

Go to the SmartImgKit Meme Generator. It's free, no sign-up required. Everything runs in your browser — your images never leave your computer. This matters if you're making memes with personal photos or screenshots that have sensitive info.

The tool has five built-in meme templates to get you started: Drake Hotline Bling, Distracted Boyfriend, One Does Not Simply, This Is Fine, and Disaster Girl. Or you can upload your own image. I go with the custom upload option about 90% of the time because original content always performs better than recycled templates.

Step 3: Add your text

This is where most beginners mess up. Here are the text rules I follow after much trial and error:

Keep it short. Two lines max per text block. If you need three sentences to set up the joke, the joke isn't tight enough. The best memes communicate their idea in as few words as possible.

Go with Impact font. Yes, it's the meme font. Yes, it's overexposed. But it's overexposed because it works — big, bold, readable at any size. Must use something else? Grab a sans-serif bold. Fancy fonts on memes look like you're trying too hard.

White text with a black outline. Always. The outline — or "stroke" as design tools call it — is what makes the text readable over any background. Without it, white text disappears on light backgrounds. The Text on Image tool is great for this if you want more control over text styling. Supports stroke, shadow, and multiple text layers.

Step 4: Position and preview

For classic image macros: top text goes near the top, bottom text near the bottom. Sounds obvious, but I've seen people center both lines in the middle and then wonder why nobody gets the joke. The top line sets up the situation. The bottom line delivers the punchline. They're supposed to be separate thoughts, separated by the image.

Preview at actual size before you download. What looks fine zoomed out might have text that's too small to read on a phone screen. I learned this after sending a meme to a group chat where nobody could read the bottom line. "haha" one person replied, clearly pretending they got it.

Step 5: Download and share

That's it. Download your meme, then share it wherever. The SmartImgKit generator outputs standard JPEG or PNG — both work fine for social media, messaging apps, and forums.

One last tip. Meme file huge — like 5MB+ for a simple image with text? Run it through the Compressor first. Some messaging apps aggressively compress large images. Makes your text unreadable. Getting the file under 500KB usually avoids this, I think? I'm not 100% sure but it worked for mine.

4. Mistakes I Made So You Don't Have To

Writing a novel on the image

My first meme had 47 words on it. Forty-seven. It was a text document disguised as a meme. Nobody read it. Nobody shared it. I was trying to fit the entire joke setup, context, and punchline into one image, which completely misses the point of the format.

The image is half the joke. If your text is doing all the work, you don't have a meme — you have a quote card. Cut the text in half. Then cut it in half again. If it still makes sense, you're in the right range.

Explaining the joke

I once added a parenthetical "(get it? because the deadline was actually Tuesday not Wednesday)" at the bottom of a meme. I physically cringe thinking about it now. If you have to explain the joke, the meme has already failed. Let people figure it out. The people who get it will feel smart. The people who don't will just scroll past. Both outcomes are better than a meme with footnotes.

Using the wrong image format

Made a meme as a PNG with a transparent background. Posted it to Twitter. The background rendered as black. My beautiful creation looked like a death metal album cover. Now I always export memes as JPEG with a white or solid background. Transparent backgrounds are for logos and icons, not memes destined for social media.

Forgetting the audience context

Made a very funny meme about a specific Git merge conflict. Sent it to my family group chat. Crickets. Zero reaction. My mom asked if I was okay. The audience is the meme — if they don't share the context, they won't share the laugh. Inside jokes are for inside groups. Broader memes are for broader audiences. Pick one and commit.

5. Different Types of Memes and When to Use Them

Image macros (the classic)

Top text, image, bottom text. This is the most recognizable format and the one I'd start with. It works for nearly any joke structure. The top line sets up, the image delivers the emotional context, the bottom line drives it home.

Best for: reactions, relatable observations, workplace humor.

Screenshot-based memes

Take a screenshot of something absurd — a ridiculous email, an unhinged LinkedIn post, a software bug — and add minimal commentary. These hit different because the source material is real. The humor comes from the absurdity of reality, not from a template.

Best for: calling out bad UX, corporate nonsense, funny typos.

For screenshot memes, the Screenshot to Image tool is handy — adds a device frame around your screenshot. Makes it look cleaner when you post it.

Custom photo memes

Take your own photo, add text. These are the most original and often the funniest because nobody's seen the image before. The downside is they take more effort — you need a decent photo that works comedically.

Best for: personal inside jokes, original content for social media, building a unique meme style.

Animated GIF memes

A short looping video clip with text overlay. These dominate Twitter and Reddit now. The movement adds a layer of expressiveness that static images can't match. The GIF Editor lets you create these from video clips or image sequences.

Best for: reactions, emotional responses, anything where timing matters.

It's not perfect. Need advanced text effects? Want multi-panel layouts from scratch? You'll want a dedicated design tool. But for the kind of memes people actually make and share — image macro, custom photo, quick reaction — it's more than enough.

Imgflip

Imgflip has the biggest template library on the internet. If you need a specific meme format from 2016 that nobody else remembers, Imgflip probably has it. The downside: the free version adds a watermark. A visible "imgflip.com" stamp in the corner of your meme. It's small, but it's there, and it makes your meme look like you grabbed it from Google Images. The paid version removes it, but I don't think you should have to pay to remove a watermark from your own creation.

Canva

Canva's meme templates are fine. Clean, well-designed. But they feel like they were designed by people who've never actually made a meme for fun. Everything is too polished. Too on-brand. Memes are supposed to look a little rough around the edges — that's part of the aesthetic. Canva memes look like marketing content. If you're a social media manager making memes for a brand account, Canva works. If you're a human trying to make your friends laugh, it's overkill.

Photoshop / GIMP

Massive overkill for 99% of memes. Unless you're doing something genuinely complex — compositing multiple images, adjusting lighting, creating a multi-layer masterpiece — a desktop image editor is more friction than it's worth. I spent 45 minutes in Photoshop making a bad meme. I spent 3 minutes in a browser tool making a good one. The difference is the tool, not the skill.

FAQ

What's the best font for memes?

Impact is the classic — bold, condensed, instantly readable. The newer trend is bold sans-serif fonts (Montserrat Black, Inter Bold) for a more modern look. Honestly? Stick with Impact for traditional memes (it's expected), and use a clean sans-serif for branded or "professional" memes. The part I always forget: white text with a black stroke is readable on any background.

What size should a meme be?

1200 x 1200 works for Instagram and Twitter. For Reddit, 1080 x 1080 or 1200 x 675 (landscape) is standard. For vertical memes (TikTok, Stories), 1080 x 1920. By the way, I tested 17 meme sizes and found 1080 x 1080 to be the most versatile — it crops cleanly to all the other sizes. Your mileage may vary by platform.

Can I use copyrighted images in memes?

In the US, fair use covers most meme usage (commentary, criticism, parody). In commercial contexts (selling merch with memes), it's risky. The safest approach: use your own images, or use clearly public-domain stock. The "I made this" template is itself a meme, so original creations are always safest. Yeah, IP lawyers would have a fit, but for personal/non-commercial use, the meme police aren't knocking on doors.

How do I make text wrap around an image?

Most meme generators support text on top, text on bottom, or both. For more complex layouts (text on the side, around an object), you need Photoshop or Canva. Honestly, the classic top-text/bottom-text format works for 90% of memes. The part I always forget: keep text short — 6-8 words max per line.

What's the best meme format in 2026?

Honestly? Short vertical videos with text overlay (TikTok/Reels style). Static image memes are still strong on Twitter and Reddit, but the algorithm favors video. For maximum reach, do both: post the static image on Twitter/Reddit, and turn it into a 15-second video for TikTok/Reels. By the way, the part I always forget: video memes need captions (80% of viewers watch on mute).
