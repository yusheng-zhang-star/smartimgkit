China Visa Photo Blue Background: Exact Hex Color & Size (2026 Rules)

China visa photos require a specific light blue background — approximately hex #438EDB. Getting the shade wrong means your visa application gets rejected. Here is the exact color, size, and how to fix your photo in 60 seconds.

The Exact Blue You Need

Light Blue — #438EDB

RGB(67, 142, 219). This is the standard blue used by Chinese embassies and visa centers worldwide.

Do not use navy blue, sky blue, or any gradient. It must be a flat, uniform, medium-light blue. The consulate scanner compares your photo against this exact color range.

Photo Size Requirements

  • Dimensions: 33mm wide × 48mm tall (1.3 × 1.9 inches)
  • Head height: 60-70% of the photo (from crown to chin)
  • Resolution: 300 DPI minimum for printing
  • File size: Under 1MB for online applications
  • Format: JPEG

What You Can and Cannot Do

✅ Allowed

  • Neutral facial expression
  • Both eyes open
  • Natural skin tone
  • Religious headwear
  • Prescription glasses (no glare)

❌ Not Allowed

  • Hats or caps
  • Sunglasses
  • Smiling with teeth
  • White or gray background
  • Shadows on face

Why getting the blue exactly right matters

A lot of people assume that as long as the background is "blue enough," the consulate will accept it — but that's how a huge number of visa applications get delayed by weeks. The automated photo scanner at Chinese visa centers checks the background color against a very narrow tolerance range: if your blue is too dark, too purple, too green, or has any gradient or texture, the system flags the photo as non-compliant, and your application gets sent back for a re-take. That means you have to wait another week for an appointment, re-print the photo, and start the process over — a hassle that's completely avoidable by using the exact standard light blue the consulates expect. You don't need to go to a dedicated photo studio that charges $20 a shot; the tool automatically uses the exact approved hex color, so the scanner reads your photo on the first try, no guessing required.

When you're taking your photo at home, lighting is the other make-or-break detail. If you stand with a window behind you, you'll end up with a dark shadow around your head and shoulders that the background remover can't fully clean up, and that shadow shows up as a dark halo on the blue background, which gets rejected. Stand facing a window, with soft natural light coming straight at your face, so your skin is evenly lit and there are no hard shadows under your chin or eyes. If you're shooting at night, turn on every overhead light in the room to get even illumination, and avoid standing under a single warm lamp, which will cast yellow tones on your skin that make your complexion look unnatural on the final ID photo. You don't need professional lighting gear — even light from a cloudy day window works perfectly, as long as there's no light coming from behind you.

One thing people get wrong when they edit their own visa photos: they smooth or alter their skin too much. The consulate expects your photo to look like a normal, unmodified picture of you — if you've used a beauty filter that changes your face shape, smooths out all your wrinkles, or adjusts your features, the border control officer may not recognize you when you arrive, which leads to issues at the airport. The whole point of an ID photo is that it looks exactly like you on a normal day. Keep the editing minimal: just fix the background, adjust the crop to the correct dimensions, and leave everything else alone. You don't need to whiten your teeth, smooth your skin, or adjust your eyebrows — the photo is meant to identify you, not to look like a social media profile picture.

Another common pitfall is printing the photo on the wrong paper type. The consulate requires a matte or semi-matte print, not glossy photo paper — glossy prints create glare when the scanner takes the photo, which throws off the color reading and causes an automatic rejection. If you're printing at home, use plain matte photo paper, and avoid printing on regular office printer paper, which is too thin and has the wrong texture that the scanner rejects. Most people who take photos at home and bring them to the visa center for the first time forget this detail, and have to go find a print shop nearby to re-print, wasting an hour of their appointment time. For people who wear prescription glasses, there's another rule that catches people out: you can't have heavy frames that cover your eyes, and there must be no glare or reflection on the lenses. If you normally wear thick plastic frames, take your glasses off for the photo, unless you're required to wear them for medical reasons. The scanner needs a clear, unobstructed view of both eyes, and any glare on the lenses creates an automatic failure. It's also fine to wear religious headwear, as long as it doesn't cover any part of your face — the consulate only requires that your full facial features are visible from the crown of your head down to your chin. You don't need to remove headscarves or other religious garments, as long as they don't cast a shadow on your face.
