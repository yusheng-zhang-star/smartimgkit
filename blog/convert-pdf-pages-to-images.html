How Local PDF to Image Conversion Works

Most online PDF converters upload your file to their cloud server, render it there, and send you back the images. That means your invoices, contracts, or unreleased catalogs are sitting on a third-party server somewhere. Our PDF to image converter uses browser-based PDF rendering:

  1. Upload your PDF file (it stays on your device)
  2. Select output format: JPG (smaller size, good for web) or PNG (sharp, transparent support)
  3. Select DPI: 150 DPI for web use, 300 DPI for print
  4. Click convert — every page renders to an image right in your browser
  5. Download all pages as a single ZIP file.
There's no upload step at all. Your sensitive documents never leave your computer.

Settings That Actually Matter

Here's what most people get wrong: they convert PDFs at 300 DPI for web use, and end up with 5MB per page. That's way too big.

Use caseRecommended DPIOutput format

At 150 DPI, a letter-sized PDF page becomes a 1240×1754px JPG, which is perfectly sharp on screen but only 200-500KB. At 300 DPI, it becomes 2480×3508px, 2-3MB per page — that's only needed if you're going to print the images.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Uploading sensitive PDFs to online converters: Contracts, invoices, tax documents all contain personal info. You have no idea what these sites do with your files after you upload them. Use local conversion.
  • Converting at too high DPI for web: 300 DPI images are huge and slow down your website. Always compress them after conversion using our image compressor.
  • Converting multi-page PDFs one page at a time: That wastes time. Our tool converts all pages at once and bundles them into a ZIP.
  • Using PNG for photo-heavy PDF pages: JPG is 5x smaller for pages with photos. Only use PNG if your PDF has text or line art that needs to stay sharp.

Why uploading PDFs to online converters is a bigger risk than you think

Most people treat online PDF converters like a utility — upload the file, get the images, done. They don't stop to think about what happens to that file after the conversion. It stays on the converter's server, often for weeks or months, and many of these services have weak security. In 2023, a popular online PDF converter was found to be storing millions of user-uploaded files on an unprotected public drive, including signed contracts, tax returns, passport scans, and business invoices. Anyone with the URL pattern could browse and download them. That's not an edge case — it's what happens when a small startup's entire business model is "upload your PDF to our server," because they have to store your file somewhere while they process it.

For sellers, this risk is concrete. If you're converting a PDF product catalog that includes unreleased SKUs, pricing, or supplier information, uploading it to a cloud converter means that sensitive business data is sitting on someone else's hard drive. Local conversion eliminates that risk entirely: the PDF is read directly from your computer, rendered in your browser, and the output images are downloaded straight to your machine. The file never touches a server at all. You can even turn off your wifi after the page loads, and the conversion still works. That's the only way to be 100% sure your private documents stay private.
