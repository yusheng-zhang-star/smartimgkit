If you've ever tried to blow up a small PNG logo to print on a t-shirt, a business card, or a large banner, you already know the problem: PNG is a raster format, made of fixed pixels. Zoom in enough and you get blurry, jagged edges. The solution is an SVG vector file, which is made of mathematical paths — it can scale from a tiny favicon to a billboard and stay perfectly sharp, with zero quality loss.

Why you need an SVG version of your logo

Every business needs vector logo files, even if they don't realize it yet. You'll run into this wall when:

  • A print shop asks for an SVG or EPS file to print your logo on banners, merchandise, or storefront signs. If you only have a PNG, they'll charge you a "logo reconstruction" fee of $50-150.
  • You're using a Cricut or vinyl cutter to make stickers or decals — these machines only cut vector paths, not raster images.
  • You want to use your logo as a favicon or app icon. SVG icons look crisp on every screen resolution, while small PNGs get blurry on retina displays.
  • You're resizing your logo for social media — an SVG exports cleanly at any dimension without re-saving.

How to convert PNG to SVG in 4 steps

Step 1: Upload your image
Drag and drop your PNG/JPG logo or icon into the converter. The image never leaves your device — the conversion runs locally in your browser.
Step 2: Adjust threshold
Use the slider to adjust the black/white threshold. If your logo has thin lines, lower the threshold; if it has heavy outlines, raise it. You'll see a live preview of the vectorized result as you slide.
Step 3: Preview vector output
Zoom in as much as you want — the SVG stays perfectly sharp at 100% zoom, because it's made of paths, not pixels. If edges look rough, tweak the threshold slider again.
Step 4: Download SVG
Save your vector file, ready to use in Illustrator, Cricut Design Space, print shops, or directly on your website.

Which images work (and don't work) for PNG to SVG conversion?

Vectorization works great on simple, high-contrast graphics, but it's not magic. Stick to these for best results:

  • Great fits: Logos, icons, line art, stickers, black-and-white graphics, simple text, geometric shapes, Cricut cut files.
  • Bad fits: Photographs, illustrations with gradients/shadows/realistic textures, complex color art, portraits. These need manual tracing in Illustrator — an automated converter will produce messy, noisy paths.

Common mistakes when vectorizing PNGs

  • Using a low-resolution source PNG. If your source logo is 100x100px and blurry, the SVG will trace the blurry edges. Start with the highest-res version of your logo you have.
  • Forgetting to adjust the threshold. The default threshold works for most images, but if your logo is light gray on white, you'll need to lower it to pick up the detail — otherwise the converter will output a blank canvas.
  • Trying to vectorize photos. A family photo will not turn into a clean SVG no matter how you adjust the slider. Use this tool for graphics, not photographs.

What you can do once you have an SVG file

A lot of people convert a PNG to SVG once, save it, and never think about it again, but vector files unlock a ton of use cases that raster files can't handle. The biggest one for small business owners is printing: if you want to put your logo on t-shirts, coffee mugs, stickers, banners, or storefront signs, the print shop will always ask for a vector file, because it can scale to any size without getting blurry. If you only have a PNG, they'll either turn you down or charge you a $100+ "redraw your logo" fee, which is ridiculous when you can do it yourself in 30 seconds with a free converter. SVG files also work perfectly for Cricut and Silhouette craft projects: vinyl cutters, sticker makers, and paper cutting machines only understand vector paths, they can't cut a raster PNG image. If you make handmade crafts, party decorations, or custom stickers, being able to turn any simple graphic or logo into an SVG means you can make your own cut files at home, instead of buying them from Etsy for $5-10 each.

SVG files are also the best way to use icons and logos on your website. Since they're text-based code files, they're tiny — a full logo SVG is usually only a few KB, compared to a PNG logo that's 50KB or more. That means your website loads faster, your pages score better on Google's Core Web Vitals, and your logo looks sharp on every screen, from a tiny phone to a 5K desktop monitor. You can even change the color of an SVG logo directly with CSS, which means you can swap it to white on dark backgrounds, or match it to your brand colors dynamically, without exporting a new file every time. If you're building a website, you should use SVG for every icon, logo, and simple graphic, and this converter lets you turn any old PNG asset you have into a proper vector file, so you don't have to recreate every icon from scratch in Illustrator.

One thing to keep in mind: auto-traced SVGs aren't perfect, and they'll have extra anchor points and rough edges compared to a logo that was drawn from scratch in Illustrator. For most use cases — printing small stickers, using icons on your website, cutting vinyl decals — the auto-traced version is more than good enough, and you'll never notice the extra rough edges. If you're using the logo for something super high-stakes, like a giant billboard or a front-page print ad, you might want to clean up the paths in a free vector editor like Inkscape afterward, but for 95% of people's everyday use cases, the direct output from the converter is perfectly fine. You also don't need any fancy software to edit an SVG: you can open it in any text editor, change colors, tweak sizes, and adjust paths directly, which is why developers love SVG so much — it's just plain code, no proprietary file format lock-in.
