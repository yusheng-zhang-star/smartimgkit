Why most online word counters are a privacy risk

When you paste text into a typical online word counter, that text is sent to the tool's server, processed there, and the count is sent back. Those servers almost always log the input: they store every paragraph, essay, draft, and note people paste, often for weeks or months. We've all seen news stories about online tools that leaked user data — a calculator tool, a PDF editor, a photo tool — where millions of user inputs were exposed in a public database. If you're writing sensitive material — a workplace complaint, a love letter, an unfinished novel, a confidential report — pasting it into a random online tool means you're sending that private content to a stranger's server, no matter what their privacy policy says.

Our word counter is a single static HTML file that runs entirely in your browser. Why word counts differ between tools

It's frustrating when you paste your 1000-word essay into our tool and it says 987 words, but Google Docs says 1012. That's not a bug — it's because different tools count words differently. Some tools count hyphenated words like "mother-in-law" as three separate words, others count it as one. Some count numbers as words, others don't. Some count URLs and email addresses as one word, others split them into multiple tokens. Our word counter follows standard APA academic counting rules, which is what most universities and academic journals expect. If your professor says your essay must be exactly 1500 words, our counter will give you the number they'll see when they grade it.
