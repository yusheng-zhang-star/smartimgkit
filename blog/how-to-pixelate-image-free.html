How to Pixelate an Image Free (Browser-Based, No Download)

Published: September 13, 2026 · 5 min read

When you need to share a screenshot or photo online, you can't just draw a black box over sensitive information — that can be erased. Pixelation is the safe way: it permanently destroys the underlying pixels so no one can read what was underneath. Our free pixelate tool does this entirely in your browser, no download, no upload required.

Step-by-Step: How to Pixelate an Image

  1. Open our pixelate tool in your browser.
  2. Upload the image you want to edit (screenshot, photo, document scan).
  3. Drag a box over the area you want to hide — a name, address, credit card number, license plate, anything.
  4. Adjust the pixel size slider: 10px is subtle, 30px is very obvious. Bigger pixels are safer — no one can guess what's under them.
  5. Add as many pixelated boxes as you need (you can pixelate multiple areas in one image).
  6. Click export, and download the pixelated image.
Your image never gets uploaded to a server. All processing happens locally on your device, so even your most sensitive screenshots stay private.

Common Use Cases

  • Sharing support screenshots: When you send screenshots to customer support, pixelate customer names, addresses, and order numbers to protect privacy.
  • Posting on social media: If you're posting a screenshot of your seller dashboard, pixelate your email, store name, and revenue numbers.
  • Redacting documents: If you're sharing an invoice, ID, or receipt, pixelate the ID number, address, and signature before sharing.
  • Covering faces: If you're sharing a photo that has people in it who didn't agree to be on camera, pixelate their faces.

Tips for Better Redaction

  • Use big pixels: If you use tiny 5px pixels, someone might be able to guess the text from the shape. Use 15-30px pixels so the shape is completely unrecognizable.
  • Check the whole image: Don't just pixelate the obvious info. Check the URL bar, the taskbar, file names, and any other places sensitive info might be hiding.
  • Export as JPG: If you export as a layered PNG, someone might be able to reverse it. Export as a flat JPG so the pixelation is permanently baked in.
  • Zoom in to check: After pixelating, zoom in on the area to make sure you covered all the text. It's easy to miss a digit or a letter.

Common Mistakes

  • Using black bars instead of pixelation: As we mentioned, black bars are not secure. Always use pixelation for sensitive info.
  • Pixelating too small an area: If you're pixelating a credit card number, make sure you cover the entire number, not just part of it. A partial number is still sensitive.
  • Uploading sensitive images to online tools: Most online pixelate tools upload your image to their server. Use our local tool so your image never leaves your device.
  • Forgetting to pixelate the URL bar: If you're taking a screenshot of a webpage, the URL in the address bar might contain sensitive info. Pixelate that too.

There's a reason we hammer on "use pixelation, not black bars" so hard: there's a well-documented case from a few years back where a major news outlet published a screenshot of an internal memo, and someone just drew a black rectangle over the supposed sensitive paragraph. It took a basic image editor about 10 seconds to erase that black box and reveal the full text, which led to a whole internal scandal. Black bars are not security. They're just a visual cover-up. Pixelation, on the other hand, actually modifies the underlying image data — once you pixelate an area, those pixels are replaced with solid blocks of color, and there's no way to recover what was originally underneath. It's the only redaction method that actually holds up when someone tries to reverse it. One thing people sometimes ask is whether AI can reverse pixelation. The answer is no, not for properly done pixelation with 15px+ block sizes. AI image restoration tools work great on blurry images, because blurring leaves most of the original pixel data intact — it's just spread out across adjacent pixels, and AI can reconstruct it. Pixelation is different: it replaces every block of pixels with one single solid color, throwing away all the detail inside that block entirely. There's no detail to reconstruct, because it's gone. As long as your pixel blocks are big enough (15px or larger), there's no AI tool on earth that can tell what was originally under that block. That's why pixelation is the standard redaction method used by courts, hospitals, and banks when they release public documents — they don't use blur, they don't use black boxes, they use pixelation, because it's the only method that actually holds up. You don't have to take our word for it: any security expert will tell you the same thing, and it's why we built our pixelation tool to use large block sizes by default, so you don't accidentally redact something in a way that can be reversed later.
