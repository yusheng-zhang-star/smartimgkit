What Old Photo Restoration Can Fix

Photos age in predictable ways, and almost every damaged family photo you pull out of a shoebox shows some combination of the same four problems:

  • Film grain and noise. Old film stocks, cheap scanners, and low-light snapshots all leave a salt-and-pepper speckle across the image. Digitally, this shows up as random brightness variation between neighboring pixels.
  • Faded contrast. Dyes in photographic paper break down over decades, and the blacks drift toward gray while whites turn cream. The histogram of a faded photo occupies only the middle of the tonal range — it looks like someone laid a gray veil over it.
  • Softness. Lens quality, focus error, scanner resolution, and paper texture all blur fine detail. Edges that were once crisp become mushy gradients.
  • Scratches and dust. Emulsion scratches, pen marks, and dust specks from scanning appear as thin bright (or dark) lines, usually running vertically because that is the direction film advanced through cameras and scanners.

Because these problems are well understood mathematically, they can be fixed with classical image processing — no neural network required, and no upload required either. That is exactly what the photo restoration tool does: six processing stages that run on the pixel data inside your browser tab, typically finishing in a second or two even for large scans.

How the Restoration Pipeline Works

When you move a slider, the tool applies a fixed sequence of operations. The order matters — removing scratches before stretching contrast, for example, prevents dust specks from being amplified into full-contrast marks. Here is what each stage actually does to your pixels:

  1. Median denoising. Each pixel is replaced with the median value of its surrounding neighborhood. The neighborhood radius grows with slider strength (roughly 1 to 3 pixels across the slider range). The median is the key choice: unlike a blur, which averages noise into the image, a median simply discards outlier specks — the odd bright pixel that grain produces — while keeping genuine edges sharp. This is why denoised photos look clean rather than watercolor-smudged, as long as you do not overdo it.
  2. Scratch removal. The algorithm compares every pixel against the average of the pixels one and two positions to its left and right. A thin vertical scratch is a pixel that differs sharply from both sides while its neighbors agree with each other — exactly the pattern this check detects. When the difference exceeds a threshold, the pixel is replaced with the neighbor average, which visually erases the line. The threshold tightens as you raise the slider, catching fainter scratches.
  3. Contrast stretching. The tool builds a histogram for each color channel, finds where the tonal range actually starts and ends (clipping a tiny fraction of statistical outliers so one stray pixel cannot skew the result), then rescales that range to the full 0–255. Faded blacks return to black, milky whites return to white, and the gray veil lifts. This single slider fixes more "my photo looks dead" complaints than everything else combined.
  4. Convolution sharpening. A standard 3×3 unsharp kernel increases the difference between each pixel and its immediate neighbors, which perceptually restores edge crispness. It amplifies whatever detail survives the earlier stages — which is why sharpen comes after denoising in the pipeline, not before.
  5. Brightness shift. A uniform adjustment that moves every pixel up or down, useful for scans that came out too dark or too light overall.
  6. Sepia toning. A color-matrix blend that mixes a warm brown tone into the image in proportion to the slider. Used lightly, it gives modern prints a period-appropriate warmth. It is a tint — more on that in the limits section.

All six stages run locally on your device. Your photo is never uploaded, which matters for family archives: many people restoring old photos are handling images of relatives, homes, and documents they would not hand to a random cloud service.

Smart Auto Restore: How It Decides

If you do not want to touch six sliders, the Smart Auto Restore button analyzes the photo and sets them for you. It samples roughly 30,000 pixels across the image and computes three measurements:

  • Noise level. It measures how much brightness changes between horizontally adjacent pixels. Grain produces many small random differences; a clean photo produces few. High readings map to stronger denoising (up to 50 on the slider), moderate readings to a gentle pass.
  • Contrast range. It measures the spread between the darkest and lightest luminance. A range under 100 signals the faded-photo gray veil, which triggers a strong contrast stretch (50); healthier ranges get a lighter touch.
  • Average brightness. It nudges the mean luminance toward a natural mid-tone, brightening dark scans and easing blown-out ones, with the adjustment capped so it can never wildly overcorrect.

Sharpening gets a fixed mild value, scratch removal starts at a conservative level, and sepia stays off. The idea is that auto gives you a sensible 80% result in one click, and the sliders cover the last 20%.

What It Cannot Fix

Honest boundaries, because every restoration tool has them:

  • No colorization. The Sepia slider tints; it does not colorize. Turning a black-and-white photo into plausible full color requires a neural network trained to predict what colors things probably were — grass green, sky blue, skin tones. This tool does not include one, and the sepia output should not be mistaken for it.
  • No rebuilding of missing areas. Scratch removal needs intact pixels on both sides of the line. Torn corners, missing emulsion, and large blotches have nothing to average from. Route these to the Object Remover, whose inpainting model fills masked regions from surrounding context.
  • No deblurring. Sharpening amplifies existing edges; it cannot reverse focus blur or camera shake. A photo that was blurry when scanned stays blurry, just crisper-blurry. For genuine resolution work, the Image Enhancer is the better starting point.
  • Strong denoise costs fine detail. Any median filter trades detail for cleanliness — that is the mechanism, not a bug. Eyelashes, fabric weave, and background texture soften as the radius grows. If a face starts looking plastic, back the slider down rather than fighting it with sharpen.
  • Horizontal scratches need a different route. The scratch detector samples left and right neighbors, so it is tuned for vertical lines — the kind film scanners produce. A horizontal scratch responds better to moderate denoising, or to painting it out in the Object Remover.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is this old photo restoration really free and private?

Yes. The restoration runs entirely in your browser with JavaScript and canvas, so your photo never leaves your device. There is no signup, no watermark, no scan quota, and no limit on how many photos you restore.

Can it colorize a black-and-white photo?

No, and any tool claiming otherwise in one click is overselling. The Sepia slider applies a warm brown tint using a standard color matrix blend — it is a tone, not real color. Genuine colorization requires a neural network trained to predict plausible colors, which this tool does not include.

What kinds of damage can it fix?

It handles the four most common types of age damage: film grain and sensor noise, faded washed-out contrast, overall softness, and thin light scratches. Each has a dedicated slider, and the Smart Auto Restore button sets all of them for you after analyzing the photo.

Can it repair torn corners or missing pieces of a photo?

No. Scratch removal replaces a pixel with the average of its horizontal neighbors, so it needs surrounding image data to work with. For missing chunks, torn corners, or large damaged regions, use the Object Remover instead — its LaMa inpainting model rebuilds missing areas from surrounding context.

What file formats and sizes does it support?

JPG, PNG, and WebP up to 20MB. Everything is processed in browser memory, so the practical limit depends on your device. The result downloads as a full-resolution PNG with no watermark.

Why does my photo look smudged after strong denoising?

The denoise slider uses a median filter, which replaces each pixel with the median value of its neighborhood. At high strength the neighborhood grows and fine detail such as eyelashes, fabric weave, or grass texture gets averaged away. Keep denoise between 15 and 50 for most photos, then restore crispness with the Sharpen slider.

How is this different from AI restoration services like MyHeritage?

AI services use neural networks to hallucinate plausible detail — enhanced faces, predicted colors — and typically require uploading your photos, an account, and a subscription for full resolution. This tool uses classical image processing that runs locally: nothing is invented, your files stay private, and usage is unlimited. The tradeoff is that it cannot recreate detail that was never captured.

Does it work on a phone or tablet?

Yes. It runs in any modern browser on Windows, Mac, Linux, Android, and iOS. There is nothing to install, and the sliders and before/after comparison work with touch input.
