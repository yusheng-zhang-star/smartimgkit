What About Apple's Built-In Background Removal?

If you've updated to iOS 16 or later, you already know Apple has a built-in feature: long-press on a subject in your Photos app, and it will lift it from the background, letting you copy or share it. It's a nice feature for quick stickers and iMessage cutouts, but it has big limitations if you're trying to make a proper product photo, ID photo, or professional headshot:

  • You can't export it as a transparent PNG to your camera roll — you can only copy it to paste into other apps, which often loses quality.
  • You can't set a custom white background for Amazon/Etsy product photos, or set a specific blue background for visa/ID photos.
  • The edge detection isn't as precise as the ISNet model used by dedicated tools, especially for fine, wispy hair or translucent objects like glass.
  • You can't batch process multiple photos — you have to long-press and save each one manually.
That's why using a dedicated tool in Safari is still the better option for actual work, even though Apple's built-in trick is fun for quick use.

Tips For Better Cutouts On iPhone

To get the cleanest possible cutout when using the tool on your iPhone:

  • Use a photo with good contrast between the subject and background — if you're cutting out a person, a plain wall or outdoor scene works better than a busy background with lots of details.
  • Make sure your subject is in focus — blurry photos make edge detection harder, and you'll get rough cutouts around hair and edges.
  • If you're cutting out fine hair, use the edge erosion slider to pull the edge in 1-2 pixels — that removes the faint halo around hair that makes cutouts look fake.
  • For product photos, use a plain white background when you take the photo — the AI will get a perfect cutout, and you won't have to spend time cleaning up edges.

Common issues people run into on iPhone

One thing that catches new users off guard is that Safari on iOS has strict limits on how much memory web pages can use. If you try to process a 12-megapixel photo straight from your iPhone's camera (which can be 4000x3000 pixels or larger), Safari might kill the tab before the model finishes processing. The fix is simple: crop or resize the photo down to 2000x2000 or smaller before you upload it. You can do this right in the Photos app's edit tool, or use the built-in image resizer on the same site. This isn't a limitation of the AI itself — it's Safari's tab memory management that's designed to keep the browser responsive on phones, and it applies to every local web AI tool, not just this one. Once you resize the photo to a reasonable size, processing is fast and reliable, even on older iPhone models.

Another common confusion: people open the tool in Chrome on their iPhone instead of Safari, and wonder why the first load takes longer. Chrome on iOS still works perfectly fine — all the same local processing happens, no uploads — it just has its own separate browser cache, so you'll need to download the AI model again the first time you open it in Chrome. If you only ever use Safari, that 30MB model download happens exactly once. If you switch between browsers, you'll get the loading screen once per browser, which is normal. There's no tradeoff in quality or privacy between Safari and Chrome on iOS — both run the exact same local WebAssembly model, and neither sends your photos anywhere.
