1. What Is HEIC, and Why Does Apple Use It?

HEIC stands for High Efficiency Image Container. Apple switched to it as the default photo format starting with iOS 11 back in 2017. The pitch was simple: HEIC files are roughly half the size of JPEGs with the same visual quality. Your 12MP iPhone photo that would've been a 3MB JPEG? It suddenly becomes a 1.5MB HEIC. Multiply that by the thousands of photos most people take and you're talking about some serious storage savings.

Apple doesn't exactly broadcast this change, by the way. Most people discover HEIC exists only when something goes wrong — a file won't upload, a program won't open it, or someone says "I can't see the photo you sent me." By that point, you've got hundreds or thousands of HEIC files sitting on your phone. And you had no idea they weren't regular JPEGs.

Look, the format itself isn't bad. It uses advanced compression based on the HEVC (H.265) video codec, which is simply more efficient than the old JPEG compression algorithm from 1992 (yeah, I know that sounds obvious). The problem isn't the technology — it's that the rest of the world hasn't caught up.

2. The Compatibility Problem Nobody Warned You About

Here's where it gets frustrating. Apple devices handle HEIC fine — Macs, iPhones, iPads, no issues. Most modern web browsers (Chrome, Firefox, Edge, Safari) can display HEIC images too. At least in recent versions they can. But step outside that ecosystem and things break down fast.

Older Windows versions? Black screen or error. Many websites that let you upload photos? "Unsupported file type." Email clients? Depends on the recipient's software. Some photo editing tools? Nope. WordPress or Squarespace? Often won't take them. Social media platforms handle them differently — Instagram accepts HEIC, but those older forums or listing sites? Definitely not.

3. How to Stop iPhone from Saving HEIC in the First Place

If you're tired of dealing with HEIC entirely, you can switch your iPhone back to JPEG. It's a simple setting change.

Go to Settings → Camera → Formats. You'll see two options: "High Efficiency" (which is HEIC) and "Most Compatible" (which is JPEG). Tap "Most Compatible" and every photo your iPhone takes from that point on will be a standard JPEG.

Well, the trade-off is storage. JPEG files are roughly twice the size of HEIC files. If you're shooting a lot of photos, you'll fill up your phone faster. On a 128GB iPhone with 10,000 photos, the difference works out to roughly 15-20GB of extra storage used. That's a big deal if you're already tight on space.

You've also got the option to keep HEIC as your default but have your iPhone automatically convert to JPEG when you share or transfer a photo. In the same Settings → Camera → Formats menu, scroll down and look for "Transfer to Mac or PC." Change it from "Automatic" to "Keep Originals" if you want the actual HEIC files, or leave it as-is if you want automatic conversion when AirDropping or cabling to a computer.

Here's what I'd go with: if you're a casual shooter who occasionally sends photos to people, switch to "Most Compatible." If you take a lot of photos and storage matters, keep HEIC and convert when you need to share. That's the approach I'll walk you through next.

5. Converting HEIC on a Mac

If you're on a Mac, you have built-in options that don't require any extra software.

Using the Preview app

The simplest method: double-click a HEIC file to open it in Preview, then go to File → Export. Choose JPEG from the format dropdown, pick a quality level, and save. That's one file done. It's fine for a few images, tedious for dozens.

Using the Photos app

Select one or more HEIC photos in the Photos app. Go to File → Export → Export [number] Photos. In the export dialog, change "Kind" to JPEG, set your quality preference, and pick a destination folder. This works for batches but has limited control over compression settings.

Using Automator (for large batches)

For bulk conversion without third-party software, you can set up an Automator workflow. Create a new Quick Action, set it to receive image files in Finder, add a "Change Type of Images" action set to JPEG, and save. Then you can right-click any HEIC file (or select multiple) and run your workflow from the Finder menu. It's a one-time setup that saves a lot of time if you're regularly converting folders of iPhone photos.

Or honestly? You can skip all of this and just grab the browser-based converter. For one-off conversions I still use Preview because it's already open. For batches of more than 5-6 files, the browser tool is faster than fiddling with Automator.

6. Converting HEIC on Windows

Windows is where HEIC causes the most headaches. Out of the box, Windows 10 and 11 don't open HEIC files without an extension.

The Microsoft HEIC extension

Open the Microsoft Store, search for "HEIF Image Extensions" (it's free, published by Microsoft), and install it. Also grab "HEVC Video Extensions" from the same publisher — it's $0.99 but some systems need both for full HEIC support. Once installed, Windows Photo Viewer and the default Photos app should handle HEIC files. Then you can open a HEIC file, use the save-as option, and pick JPEG.

This works. But it's clunky for batch conversions, and some older Windows installations have trouble with the extension. I've seen it work perfectly on one machine and fail silently on another with the same Windows version (no idea why).

Using PowerShell (for tech-savvy folks)

If you have the HEIF extension installed and want to convert a whole folder without clicking through a GUI, you can script it with PowerShell. It's not the most user-friendly approach, but it's effective for IT folks or anyone who regularly processes batches of iPhone photos on a Windows machine.

The browser alternative

Honestly, for most Windows users, the browser converter is the path of least resistance. No Store extensions to install, no compatibility headaches, no wondering why one HEIC opens and another doesn't. Drag, convert, download. Done.

7. Does Converting HEIC to JPEG Kill Quality?

So here's the thing — it depends. But for most practical purposes? No.

HEIC is a lossy format, same as JPEG. When you convert HEIC to JPEG, you're re-compressing an already-compressed image. In theory, each generation of lossy compression degrades quality. In practice, the quality loss from HEIC-to-JPEG conversion is invisible to the naked eye in most cases.

Where you might notice it: heavy cropping or zooming into fine details (like text in a photo, fabric texture, or distant small objects). If you're converting photos for social media, web use, or email, the quality difference is a non-issue. If you're converting for print at large sizes, or for professional photography work where every pixel matters, you might want to stick with HEIC-compatible workflows or convert to PNG instead of JPEG to avoid additional compression.

The quality slider on the converter tool lets you control the trade-off. At 95% quality, a HEIC-to-JPEG conversion is virtually indistinguishable from the original. At 70%, you might start seeing artifacts in smooth gradients like sky or skin tones. I generally land around 85% — good enough for websites and client delivery, with reasonable file sizes.

When NOT to convert HEIC to JPEG

If you're a pro photographer archiving RAW files or need maximum quality for large-format print, keep the HEIC originals — converting to JPEG throws away data permanently. This guide is for people who just need a quick shareable version, not for archival workflows.

Also, if you're on a Mac and only sharing with other Apple users, there's honestly no rush to convert. They can already open HEIC natively. Spend your time on something else. Your mileage may vary, but if you hit any of these cases, stop and reconsider.

FAQ

Can Android phones open HEIC files?

Modern Android phones (Android 9 and newer with recent Google Photos updates) can generally handle HEIC. Older Android devices usually can't. If you're sending a photo to someone and you're not sure what phone they have, convert to JPEG first. It takes like 5 seconds and saves an awkward "I can't open this" reply.

Is HEIC better than JPEG?

Technically yes — HEIC achieves better compression at similar visual quality, supports 16-bit color (vs JPEG's 8-bit), and can store depth maps and motion data alongside the image. But "better" doesn't mean "more compatible." JPEG is the universal standard for a reason: it works everywhere. HEIC is better in theory. JPEG is better in practice when you need to share files with other people, upload them to websites, or use them in workflows that weren't designed with Apple's ecosystem in mind.

Will I lose EXIF data when converting?

Not with the SmartImgKit converter — it preserves EXIF metadata (camera model, date, location, etc.) during conversion. Some online converters strip EXIF data, which is a big deal if you need to preserve timestamps or location info. Browser-based tools that process locally tend to be better about this since they're not optimizing for server storage costs.

Can I convert HEIC to PNG instead of JPEG?

Absolutely, and the same converter tool supports it. PNG is lossless, so you avoid any additional compression artifacts. The trade-off is file size — a PNG converted from a 12MP HEIC can easily be 8-15MB, while the JPEG equivalent might be 2-4MB. Go with PNG when you need maximum quality (archival, print, transparent backgrounds) and JPEG when file size matters (web, email, social media).

Why do my HEIC photos look different when converted?

HEIC supports a wider color gamut than standard JPEG. iPhone photos shot in HEIC often use the Display P3 color space, which is wider than sRGB. When you convert to JPEG, the image gets mapped to sRGB, which can make colors look slightly less vibrant. Most people don't notice this unless they're comparing the two side by side. For critical color work, convert to PNG or use software that supports P3-to-sRGB mapping with perceptual rendering intent.

How do I convert HEIC files on my iPhone itself?

Honestly? The easiest method on iOS: open the HEIC photo, tap the Share button, and choose "Save to Files." This saves it as-is. For actual conversion, your best bet is to open the converter in Safari, upload the photo from your camera roll, and download the JPEG. Alternatively, you can use the Shortcuts app to create an auto-conversion workflow — there are pre-made shortcuts for this on the Gallery. Anyway, switching to "Most Compatible" in Camera formats is the permanent fix. This worked for me, but you might find a different way.
