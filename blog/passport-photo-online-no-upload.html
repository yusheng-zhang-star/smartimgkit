Official Passport Photo Requirements (US)

  • Size: 2x2 inches (51x51mm)
  • Background: Plain white or off-white, no patterns
  • Expression: Neutral face, mouth closed, looking directly at the camera
  • Glasses: No glasses (even prescription ones, unless you have a medical reason)
  • Head coverings: Only for religious reasons, and your entire face must be visible
  • Shadows: No shadows on your face or behind you
Our tool automatically checks that your photo meets these requirements, and tells you if anything needs to be fixed.

How to Take a Good Passport Photo at Home

  • Stand 6 feet from the wall: This avoids shadows on the wall behind you.
  • Use natural light: Stand facing a window, with light coming from in front of you. No overhead lights — they create shadows under your eyes.
  • Take the photo at eye level: Don't hold the phone above your head — hold it at eye level, straight on.
  • Use the timer: Set the phone timer to 10 seconds so you can stand back and frame yourself correctly.
  • Wear dark clothes: Against a white background, dark clothes look better and don't blend in.

Common Mistakes

  • Smiling: US passport photos require a neutral expression, no smile. If you smile, your photo will get rejected.
  • Wearing glasses: Even prescription glasses are not allowed in US passport photos. Take them off for the photo.
  • Shadows on the face: If you use overhead lighting, you'll have shadows under your eyes and chin. Use front-facing natural light instead.
  • Printing on plain paper: Regular printer paper has a yellowish tint, and the background won't be pure white. Use glossy photo paper.
  • Uploading photos to random online tools: Most online passport photo tools upload your photo to their server. Our tool runs locally, so your personal photo never leaves your device.

Why local processing matters for passport photos

Most free online passport photo tools ask you to upload your photo to their servers to process it — that means your biometric portrait, a picture of your face that's used to verify your identity for border control, gets stored on a random third-party server somewhere. You have no idea who runs that service, how long they keep your photo, who they sell it to, or if it gets leaked in a data breach. Passport photos are sensitive personal information: your face, your age, your likeness, all in a high-resolution image that's perfect for identity theft or deepfake training. When the tool processes your photo entirely in your browser, that image data never leaves your laptop or phone at all — there's no upload request sent to any server, even if you disconnect your internet after loading the page, the cropping and formatting still works. That's the level of privacy you should expect when you're handling official identity documents, and it's why local processing isn't just a nice feature, it's the minimum bar for a tool that handles your face.

Printing at home is cheaper, but there are a couple of small details people miss. First, make sure your printer settings are set to "actual size" or "100% scale," not "fit to page" or "scale to fill." If you accidentally let the printer scale the image to fit the paper, your 2x2 inch photo will come out 1.9 inches or 2.1 inches, which is outside the acceptable tolerance, and the passport office will reject it. Second, set your paper type to "glossy photo paper" in the printer settings, not plain paper — this tells the printer to use the right ink density, so the white background comes out pure white instead of slightly gray. If you don't have a photo printer at home, just save the 4x6 composite image to your phone and send it to the kiosk at CVS, Walgreens, or any pharmacy that prints 4x6 photo prints. It costs 10-30 cents, and you get the same glossy print you'd get if you paid $15 for their in-store passport photo service, the only difference is you took the picture yourself.

One thing that catches a lot of people off guard: the head height requirement. The US State Department requires your head (from the top of your hair to the bottom of your chin) to measure between 1 and 1 and 3/8 inches on the final 2x2 inch photo. Most people take a selfie from too far away, so their head comes out too small in the frame, and the photo gets rejected even if the background is white and the expression is neutral. The tool automatically crops your photo to meet this head height rule, but when you're taking the original shot, stand about arm's length from your phone, make sure the frame cuts off right above your shoulders, and you'll end up with the right proportions. If you're not sure, take a few shots at different distances, and use the one where your face takes up roughly two-thirds of the frame.
