Turn a sequence of images into a looping GIF

GIFs are the most durable format on the internet. From reaction memes and chat stickers to product demos and tutorial steps, the GIF plays everywhere — every chat app, every social feed, every browser — without a player, without a codec, without a click. GIF Maker assembles a sequence of images into a single animated GIF, with control over frame delay, looping, and output size, no Photoshop required.

Compared to video, the GIF's superpower is frictionless playback. A three-second GIF of a feature demo often gets watched all the way through where a thirty-second video gets skipped. For short, loopable, "show don't tell" moments, the GIF is still the right tool.

Who uses it

  • Marketers and social media managers — turn screenshots into product demos and step-by-step tutorials.
  • Developers — add a short feature demo to a README or docs page.
  • Designers — preview frame-by-frame animation or prototype lightweight motion.
  • Everyone else — make reaction GIFs, stickers, and keepsake mini-animations.

How to use it

  1. Upload images in the order you want them to play — order matters.
  2. Set the frame delay; smaller numbers mean faster motion.
  3. Choose whether it loops forever or plays once.
  4. Pick the output width, generate, and download.

Frame delay controls the rhythm

Around 100ms per frame gives product demos a clear, readable pace, while 50ms works for rapid, snappy transitions. The more frames you use and the shorter the delay, the smoother the animation — but also the larger the file. For a demo GIF, err toward slower and clearer rather than faster and blurrier.

GIF is capped at 256 colors

This is a hard limit of the format, not a bug. Photographs and smooth gradients often show banding when converted to GIF. Flat colors, simple illustrations, and solid UI elements convert beautifully, which is why GIFs are ideal for screenshots and interface demos and less ideal for full-color photography. If you need richer color, consider APNG or a short looping video instead.

Keep the file size in check

GIFs have none of the efficient compression of modern video codecs, so frame count, dimensions, and color complexity all inflate the file size quickly. For a demo GIF, stay around 600px wide and roughly 20 frames — that is the sweet spot between clarity and load speed. If your animation is longer than a few seconds, a short MP4 or WebM is almost always the better choice.

Order your frames before you upload

Upload order is playback order, so name your files sequentially (01, 02, 03…) before you start. A frame out of order produces a jarring jump in the loop that is easy to miss until after you have already shared it.

What this tool does not do

This tool combines still images into a GIF. It does not convert video to GIF, and it does not let you edit individual frame content. If you need per-frame edits, do that in an editor first, then assemble the frames here. All processing happens locally in your browser.

The GIF is still unmatched for "show, don't tell"

Even in an era of autoplaying video, the GIF has a unique role: it plays instantly, loops forever, and needs no sound, no player, and no click. That makes it the ideal format for a quick UI interaction, a reaction, or a three-step how-to embedded in a chat, a doc, or a README. A short GIF communicates a motion that a screenshot cannot and that a full video overcomplicates.

The key is brevity. A GIF earns its keep when it is a few seconds long and loops naturally. Once an animation stretches beyond a few seconds or needs real detail, a looping video is almost always the better trade-off. Knowing where that line is will save you a lot of oversized, blurry files.
