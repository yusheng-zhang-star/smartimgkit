Find photos that are visually the same

Over time, every photo library accumulates near-identical shots — burst photos of the same moment, the same image saved to three folders, downloads repeated by accident. Duplicate Image Finder uses perceptual hashing (pHash) to identify images that look the same, so you can see at a glance which files are true duplicates and which are merely "similar but different."

Unlike comparing filenames or file sizes, perceptual hashing looks at visual content. Two images can differ in resolution, format, or even be slightly cropped or recompressed — if the picture looks the same, the hashes match. That makes it genuinely useful for cleaning up a phone gallery, organizing an asset library, or checking a client delivery for duplicates.

Who uses it

  • Photographers — clear out burst and bracketed shots that are visually redundant.
  • Designers and asset managers — dedupe a library of re-downloaded stock and assets.
  • Casual users — reclaim storage by finding repeated photos on a phone or drive.
  • E-commerce operators — tidy product image libraries where one image got saved under several names.

How to use it

  1. Select or drop in the images you want to compare.
  2. The tool computes a perceptual hash for each and compares visual similarity.
  3. Results are grouped by similarity, flagging likely duplicates.
  4. Review the groups and delete the extras yourself — the tool never deletes anything automatically.

Tune the threshold to your goal

If you only want to catch pixel-identical files, use a high threshold. If you also want to surface images that were cropped, recompressed, or lightly filtered, lower it. Go too low, though, and you will start matching different compositions that happen to share a scene — so always eyeball the results before deleting.

Start with burst photos

Burst mode and exposure bracketing are the biggest sources of "almost identical" photos, and therefore the highest-value batch to clean first. A few minutes removing those redundant frames often reclaims the most storage for the least effort.

Double-check before you delete

Perceptual hashing judges overall visual similarity, not intent. Two images that look alike might serve different purposes — a full-resolution master and a compressed web version, for example. Glance at the dimensions before you remove anything, and remember the tool only suggests groups; the final delete is always your call.

What this tool does not do

Perceptual hashing excels at "whole image looks alike" and is weaker at "same scene, different subject." It offers similarity groupings, not automatic deletion, and everything runs locally in your browser — your images are never uploaded.

A cleanup habit beats a big cleanup

Photo libraries tend to grow faster than we clean them, which is why a one-time purge rarely sticks. The better approach is a light, recurring habit: every month or so, run a duplicate scan, remove the true repeats, and delete the obvious throwaways. Ten minutes of maintenance keeps a library tidy far more reliably than an annual marathon session.

This is especially valuable if you pay for cloud storage by the gigabyte. Duplicate and near-duplicate files quietly consume a meaningful share of that paid space, so a quick scan is effectively a small recurring saving. For photographers and asset-heavy creators, the habit pays for itself quickly.
