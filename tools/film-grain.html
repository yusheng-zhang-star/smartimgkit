Film Grain Filter: Add realistic analog grain and noise to photos

Film Grain adds authentic analog-style noise to any photo in one click, entirely in your browser. Move a single intensity slider and watch the grain render live on canvas — no upload, no sign-up, no watermark.

🎞️

Recreate a vintage film look

Digital photos look sterile next to film. Adding subtle grain reintroduces the organic texture of a 35mm scan, which is why grain is the go-to for retro edits and lo-fi album art.

🌫️

Hide banding and digital noise

A small amount of grain masks the harsh posterisation and banding that appears in smooth gradients like skies and studio backdrops, making them look smoother rather than noisier.

🖼️

Blend composite elements

When you drop a cut-out object onto a new background, grain helps the two layers match by giving the whole image one consistent noise floor — the same trick professional retouchers use.

Detailed User Guide

Step 1: Upload Your Image

Click the drop zone or drag in a JPG, PNG or WebP. The image loads onto the canvas instantly and the current grain level is applied immediately.

Step 2: Adjust the Grain Intensity

The intensity slider controls how much random noise is added. At 5–15% you get a barely-there film texture; at 40%+ it reads as heavy analog grain. Each pixel's red, green and blue channels are nudged by a random amount scaled to your setting, so the grain stays colour-neutral rather than tinting the image.

Step 3: Download Your Result

Click Download Grainy Photo to export a JPG. Because processing is local, a large image may take a moment to render — the result is always full resolution.

What Grain Intensity Works Best?

For portraits, keep intensity low (under 20%) so skin stays clean. For street photography or a documentary feel, 20–40% adds grit without obscuring detail. Push past 50% only when you want a deliberate, heavily-textured lo-fi effect.

How the Grain Is Generated

For every pixel, a random value is drawn and scaled by your intensity setting, then added to all three colour channels at once. Because the same offset is applied to red, green and blue, the noise stays neutral grey rather than introducing a colour cast — this is what keeps the effect looking like real film grain instead of a rainbow of speckles.

Troubleshooting and Limitations

If the preview looks slow on a very large photo, that is expected: the tool touches every pixel in memory, so a 40-megapixel file takes longer than a phone snapshot. The grain is uniform across the image, so there is no way to confine it to a region within this tool — for that, apply grain and then composite with another tool. The effect is applied once on export, so re-opening the JPG and adding more grain will stack additional noise rather than refine the first pass.

Real-World Uses

Photographers add a light grain to digital work destined for print so it matches the texture of scanned film in a mixed portfolio. Social creators use a subtle grain to unify photos taken on different phones into one consistent feed. Designers add heavy grain to hero images for a gritty, editorial or album-art look where clean pixels would feel too clinical.
