Batch Old Photo Colorizer

This tool applies a warm sepia tone to black-and-white photos, one at a time or in a batch, by running each pixel through a fixed luminance-to-colour curve. It does not identify objects and it does not assign colours — a sky will not become blue and grass will not become green. What it does well is make a set of scans share one consistent warm tone, which is the look most people are after when they say a photo should feel restored.

What happens to each pixel

The loop reads one value out of every pixel — the red channel — and treats it as brightness. That is a deliberate shortcut rather than a mistake worth hiding: for a true greyscale scan all three channels are equal, so the red channel is the grey value. On a photo that still carries a colour cast, the red channel sits higher than the real luminance, and the whole image is treated as brighter than it is. If your source is a tinted or faded scan rather than a clean greyscale file, convert it to greyscale first for predictable results.

That single value then picks one of three branches, and each branch multiplies the channels by a different amount:

  • Above 200 (highlights). Red gains 20, green 10 and blue 30, so bright areas drift towards a cool, paper-white tone instead of turning cream.
  • Between 120 and 200 (midtones). Red is multiplied by 1.1, green by 0.9 and blue by 0.8 — the strongest warm push, which is where most of the sepia character comes from.
  • At or below 120 (shadows). Red by 1.05, green by 0.95, blue by 0.85, a gentler tilt so dark areas keep their depth instead of going muddy red.

Each pixel is handled in isolation. There is no neighbour sampling, no edge detection and no notion of what the pixel represents, which is exactly why the output is a tint rather than a colourisation.

Why a warm tone reads as “restored”

Silver-based prints fade unevenly: the cyan and blue dyes go first, leaving the yellow and red to dominate. A century-old family photo therefore looks brown-gold not because that was the scene, but because that is what survived. Viewers have learned the association, so adding warmth back into a flat grey scan produces the same recognition a genuine aged print triggers.

This is a real effect, and it is worth being precise about what it delivers: a coherent period look, quickly, across a whole folder of scans. It is also why the tool is popular for reunions, memorial pages and family-history books, where the goal is that forty photographs from four different scanners look like they belong together. The tone curve gives you exactly one look, applied identically to every file, which is the property that makes a batch coherent in the first place.

Where the tone curve stops, and what real colorization needs

Assigning plausible colour requires knowing what things are: sky at the top of the frame, skin in the middle, foliage at the edges. That is a semantic problem, and solving it needs a trained model that has seen millions of photographs and can guess which hue belongs where. Such models exist and can be impressive on portraits and street scenes, but they guess rather than know — a brown coat may come back blue, and the same photo colorized twice can disagree with itself. This page does not do that, and adding a tint should never be described as colorizing.

Other things this page does not do, so you are not surprised later: it does not remove dust, scratches or fold lines (those need retouching, with a healing brush or an inpainting tool); it does not increase sharpness; and it does not recover detail from a blown-out or crushed scan. Its export is JPEG at quality 0.9, which re-encodes the image and drops the original EXIF block, so if the capture date matters, keep your source file. Dimensions are preserved, so nothing is resampled.

A practical order of operations for restoring a family archive: scan at the highest optical resolution you have, spot the dust and scratches first, adjust the tonal range so the shadows are not clipped, and only then apply the warm tone. Tinting before retouching makes every scratch easier to see and harder to remove, because the tone curve amplifies the contrast around them.
