Compress an image to 150 KB without sending it anywhere

This page does one job: get your file to 150 KB or less. The target-size field is already set to 150, so you only have to pick a file. The compression runs inside your browser — nothing is uploaded and no account is needed.

📋

Where the 150 KB limit comes from

Document upload screens on government and institutional portals commonly land on 100–200 KB, with 150 KB appearing as an explicit maximum. Some school and scholarship submission portals accept scanned pages only up to 150 KB each. Certain ticketing systems cap per-image payload at 150 KB to keep database blobs small.

⚠️

Why the file keeps going over 150 KB

Multi-page scans saved as one giant PNG — that is both the wrong format and far too many pixels. Photographing a document at close range on a modern phone, producing a 12 MP file. Assuming the browser will shrink the file for you. It will not; upload limits are checked on the raw bytes.

🔒

Private by design

There is no upload step and no server round trip. The image is decoded, re-encoded and offered back to you as a download, all inside the page. That also means it keeps working when a locked-down portal or an inbox is the only thing you have access to.

Compressing to 150 KB in Detail

Note on mechanics: the tool re-encodes your file at up to eight JPEG/WebP quality levels between roughly 10% and 95% and keeps the smallest result that lands at or under 150 KB. It never changes pixel dimensions. For scanned pages at 150 KB the search generally lands mid-range, which is why text usually stays readable.

Where 150 KB shows up

This figure is common on document workflows rather than photo workflows: scanned pages, certificates, receipts and signed forms. It is large enough that a full page of text survives comfortably, and small enough that a batch of them does not stress a document store. If a form mentions both a dimension requirement and a 150 KB cap, the size is the binding constraint in practice.

Why text compresses well and photos do not

Text on a flat background has enormous redundancy — long runs of identical white pixels cost almost nothing after encoding. That is why a readable 1500 px scanned page fits comfortably in 150 KB while a 1500 px photograph of a forest struggles. When a limit feels unfair, it is usually because the content is photographic rather than textual.

Checklist for scanned documents at 150 KB

  • Scan or capture at 1500–2000 px on the long edge; more is wasted on text.
  • Output as JPG, not PNG — a scanned page as PNG can be ten times the size.
  • Straighten and crop to the page edge so no dark background is encoded.
  • Check the preview for legibility before submitting.

Keeping text readable as the size drops

Legibility falls off a cliff once character strokes start merging, and that happens later than most people expect: a 1500 px page at 150 KB is usually perfectly readable, while the same page at 40 KB may not be. If a portal also accepts a higher limit such as 200 KB, take it — the extra 50 KB goes entirely into stroke definition and costs nothing else.
